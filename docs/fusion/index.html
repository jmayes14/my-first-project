Material: lithium

Fusion runs on lithium nobody can trace yet.

A fusion power plant burns tritium, which barely exists in nature. So reactors have to make their own, by hitting lithium-6 with neutrons in a "breeder blanket." This page follows that lithium from salt flats and hard-rock mines, through refineries and isotope plants, into the reactor, and shows who could be harmed along the way.

How to read this

Every claim links to its source. Reported allegations are labeled as such. A trade link from Sayari shows that two organizations traded, not what was in the box. Gaps are marked, and a gap is not evidence that a chain is clean or harmful.

    Deliverable 3 · Policy brief and tech proposal

    Build fusion's lithium chain clean before it scales

    The ask: make public fusion money conditional on knowing where the lithium came from, and fund a lithium-6 supply that does not use mercury or weapons plants.

    The problem

    Fusion needs little lithium by battery standards, but it needs a special kind. One power-plant blanket holds about 100 tonnes of lithium-6, and almost all of today's enrichment capacity sits inside nuclear-weapons programs in Russia and China that use a mercury process. The last time the United States did it at scale, Oak Ridge lost hundreds of tonnes of mercury into the land and a creek, and the cleanup is still going. Upstream, the same lithium passes through salt flats where Indigenous communities say they were not consulted, Zimbabwean mines on the U.S. child-labor list, and Chinese refining that the U.S. now treats as a forced-labor priority.

    Why now

    Commercial fusion is roughly a decade from needing tonnes of lithium-6. Supply chains are cheapest to shape before contracts and plants lock them in. Pilot plants in the U.S., U.K., and Europe are already signing material deals, and much of the money is public.

    Three recommendations

    1. Put conditions on public fusion funding

    Any DOE, UKAEA, or ITER-funded project that buys lithium or breeder material should report the mine or brine of origin, the refiner, and the enrichment plant, and show due diligence that follows the OECD guidance for minerals, which applies to all minerals, not only conflict minerals. Brine sources should show evidence of free, prior and informed consent.

    Who acts: DOE Office of Science and fusion milestone programs; UKAEA (LIBRTI); ITER Organization procurement.

    2. Fund mercury-free lithium-6, and keep public money away from mercury and weapons plants

    Use the DOE Isotope Program and grants to scale cleaner methods such as chromatography, crown-ether exchange, and electrochemical separation, which companies like Precision Periodic and Molten Salt Solutions are developing. Do not buy enriched lithium made by COLEX or by plants inside weapons complexes, in line with the Minamata Convention on mercury.

    Who acts: DOE Isotope Program; Congress (appropriations); U.K. Department for Energy Security and Net Zero.

    3. Close the isotope gap in trade rules

    Make clear that UFLPA lithium enforcement covers enriched lithium and lithium compounds, and add an origin declaration for lithium isotopes at import. This blocks a route where Xinjiang-linked or Russian lithium reaches fusion labs through isotope distributors.

    Who acts: Forced Labor Enforcement Task Force (DHS); U.S. Customs and Border Protection; Commerce (export controls).

    The tech piece: a Fusion Lithium Passport

    A shared record that travels with each batch of lithium, from mine to blanket. Each step adds who handled it and what they checked. Before a contract is signed, the buyer runs the companies through ownership and trade checks in a tool like Sayari, which is exactly what this page does by hand.

    Mine or brineLocation, owner, consent evidence, labor audit
    RefinerPlant, owner, Xinjiang screening
    EnrichmentMethod (no mercury), plant, owner
    Breeder makerMaterial, enrichment level, batch
    ReactorProject, public funder, recycle plan

    It should also include a complaint channel, so affected communities can attach evidence to a batch record, not just companies.

    Limits and trade-offs

    • Conditions add cost and could slow early experiments. A phase-in for research quantities, under about 1 kg, would limit that.
    • A passport only works if refiners and enrichers take part. Public funding is the lever, because most fusion buyers today are publicly funded.
    • Tracing tools see ownership and trade, not working conditions. Audits and community voice are still needed.
    • Lithium-6 enrichment is export-controlled because of weapons, so some records will need restricted access.

    How we would know it worked

    • Share of publicly funded fusion lithium with a known mine of origin.
    • Tonnes of lithium-6 made without mercury outside weapons programs.
    • Complaints filed by communities and how they were resolved.
